A beginner-friendly reference covering 11 body systems, 22 distinct remedies, six core herbs, and conservative adult and child use.

This guide brings together traditional herbal practice, herbal medicine-making methods, botanical safety references, and relevant clinical research.

Important distinction: These are home preparations for comfort, nutrition, and limited symptom support. They are not substitutes for medical treatment, and not every traditional herbal use has been demonstrated in human clinical trials.

The remedies below are deliberately mild. Their exact household recipes and short trial periods are conservative adaptations of published preparation methods, not necessarily formulations tested in clinical studies.

For evidence, I use these distinctions:

  • [Verified] A finding or safety statement supported by a source I directly checked.
  • [Traditional] A use described in herbal practice but not necessarily established by clinical research.
  • [Inference] A cautious practical adaptation, such as choosing a smaller serving or shorter duration than a published adult herbal dose.

I reviewed the American Herbal Products Association's Botanical Safety Handbook, Michael Moore's Herbal Materia Medica, Jim McDonald's preparation guidance, Aviva Romm's pediatric herbal guidance, and additional relevant sources. I could not independently verify the full contents of every requested book, so I have not attributed specific doses to books I could not examine.

2. Where to start: your first five remedies

Start with one remedy at a time. This makes it easier to learn the preparation method and recognize any unwanted reaction.

1. Ginger settling decoction

A simple way to learn decoctions using fresh roots. Useful as a warm drink for occasional mild digestive discomfort.

2. Chamomile bedtime tea

Introduces covered infusions using a readily available flower. Traditionally used for relaxation and mild digestive tension.

3. Garlic-lemon broth

A practical introduction to food-based herbalism, without concentrated extracts or complicated equipment.

4. Soothing oat bath

A gentle topical preparation for dry, itchy, intact skin. No herbal extracts are swallowed.

5. Calendula skin salve

Teaches infused oils and salve-making in one project. A useful next step once you've practiced simple teas.

Suggested first-week sequence: Make the ginger or chamomile tea first, the broth on another day, and the oat bath when needed. Prepare the calendula salve as your first longer project. There is no benefit to taking all five simultaneously.

For children, pregnancy, breastfeeding, chronic illness, or regular medication use, follow the individual restrictions below rather than assuming that a remedy is safe because its ingredients are familiar.

3. Beginner herbal preparation primer

The basic techniques behind the recipes

The most important skill is choosing the right preparation for the plant material. Soft leaves and flowers usually need gentle steeping. Dense roots and seeds often need more time or heat.

Infusion (herbal tea)

Pour 240 mL recently boiled water over approximately 1–2 g of dried leaves or flowers. Cover for 10–20 minutes, then strain. Keeping the lid on helps retain aromatic compounds.

Decoction

Place approximately 2–4 g of chopped fresh root or an herb-specific amount of dried root in 240 mL water. Simmer gently for 10–15 minutes. Strain. Strength varies considerably by herb.

Tincture

An alcohol-based extract. A common starting ratio for compatible dried herbs is 1:5 weight to volume: 20 g dried herb with 100 mL suitable alcohol-water mixture, often 40–60% alcohol. Macerate 2–6 weeks, then strain. The required alcohol strength is herb-specific.

Glycerite

An alcohol-free herbal extract made with food-grade vegetable glycerin. An illustrative dried-herb formula uses 20 g dried herb, 60 mL glycerin, and 40 mL water. Macerate approximately 3–4 weeks and strain. Not every herb extracts well this way.

Oxymel

An extract using honey and vinegar, often in roughly equal volumes. For example, 120 mL honey and 120 mL vinegar can be used with a suitable dried herb, then strained after 2–4 weeks. Acidity, sugar content, and herb choice affect tolerability and storage.

Infused oil and salve

Infuse completely dried herbs in olive oil over gentle indirect heat, then strain. For a soft salve, combine roughly 100 mL infused oil with 8–12 g beeswax and melt together gently. Let cool in a clean tin.

For syrups, a common household technique combines strained herbal tea and honey in roughly equal volumes. Refrigerate and make small batches.

Preparation safety

  • Use a scale for herbs rather than relying on teaspoons when quantities matter. Spoon weights vary by grind and density.
  • Keep fresh herbal teas refrigerated and use within 24 hours as a conservative household rule.
  • Cold infusions should steep in the refrigerator, rather than sitting in warm conditions overnight.
  • Make infused oils only from fully dried herbs for these recipes. Introducing water into oil increases spoilage risks. Never store homemade raw-garlic-in-oil preparations at room temperature.
  • Homemade glycerites, oxymels, and syrups do not have automatically validated shelf lives. Without validated preservation, refrigerate small batches and discard anything with gas, mold, fermentation, or unusual odor.
  • Alcohol tincture doses cannot be transferred directly to glycerites. Extraction strength and absorption may differ.
  • Do not give homemade tinctures or glycerites to children based on a general age or weight formula alone.

These techniques are grounded chiefly in Jim McDonald's Herbal Preparations, James Green's The Herbal Medicine-Maker's Handbook, and Michael Moore's Herbal Materia Medica.

Understanding pediatric dosing

Aviva Romm discusses gentle teas made from ginger, chamomile, lemon balm, and elderflowers for children and describes approximately ¼–1 cup as a broad serving range for toddlers and older children. That is not a universal dose for every herb or every tea concentration.

For those four herbs, I have used the lower end of her range for age 2–6 and a modest serving for age 6–12, with weaker preparations where specified. These are conservative adaptations, not doses clinically validated for the exact recipe.

I have not applied Clark's or Young's rule to the other botanicals. Dividing an adult dose by weight cannot establish that a particular herb is safe for a child.

Children under 2: None of the medicinal remedies in this guide is recommended for unsupervised use at this age. Honey is specifically unsafe under 12 months because of infant botulism. Children with illness should receive age-appropriate fluids, foods, and pediatric care as needed.

4. The six core-herb profiles

Garlic (Allium sativum)

Part used: Bulb or cloves.

Traditional energetics: Strongly warming and drying.

Main actions: Pungent digestive stimulant, culinary antimicrobial activity, and traditional respiratory and circulatory support. Laboratory antimicrobial effects do not establish that eating garlic treats an infection in a person.

Best forms: Crushed fresh garlic, cooked garlic, broth, and food. Concentrated extracts behave differently.

Preparation tip: Crush or chop garlic and allow it to rest approximately 10 minutes before cooking. This allows alliinase to produce allicin before heat inactivates the enzyme.

Conservative food portions: Adults, ¼–1 clove (approximately 0.75–3 g); ages 2–6, about ⅛ clove (0.4 g) cooked in food; ages 6–12, about ¼ clove (0.75 g) in food. These are illustrative portions, not established medical daily doses.

Safety: [Verified] The AHPA handbook identifies clinically relevant interaction concerns for higher garlic intakes, including bleeding-related precautions, an interaction with saquinavir, irritation from raw garlic, reflux aggravation, and possible chemical burns when raw garlic is placed on skin. Discuss medicinal use before surgery; the handbook advises a seven-day preoperative stop for garlic use of concern. Pregnancy and breastfeeding are different considerations at food amounts than at supplemental doses.

Pairs with: Lemon, thyme, cooked vegetables, olive oil.

Avoid combining medicinal-strength preparations with: Anticoagulants, antiplatelet medicines, or other agents affecting bleeding without professional review. Avoid raw-garlic poultices.

From the tradition Added

Rosalee de la Forêt describes garlic as warming and drying, and says herbalists commonly use it for symptoms of upper respiratory infections, as a general antimicrobial, and to support heart health and digestion. She lists food, oil, vinegar, and honey among ordinary preparations. Source: Rosalee de la Forêt, “Garlic Uses and Plant Profile,” LearningHerbs, 14 July 2021, learningherbs.com/blog/garlic-uses.

Felter and Lloyd, in King’s American Dispensatory (1898), call garlic stimulant, diuretic, expectorant, and rubefacient, and record its traditional use in coughs and catarrhal affections. They give a historical adult dose of 1 or 2 drachms of fresh garlic, or a small teaspoonful of the juice, and warn that large doses can cause nausea, vomiting, and purging. A traditional syrup of garlic is described separately for cough and chronic catarrhal affections. Source: henriettes-herb.com/eclectic/kings/allium-sati.html and the syrup monograph at allium-sati_syru.html.

Paul Bergner’s practitioner interviews in The Healing Power of Garlic, excerpted in Medical Herbalism, record Mary Bove’s clinical use of a garlic syrup in most of her respiratory formulas, with glycerites rather than alcohol tinctures for children, and her observation that “Garlic is really great for the lungs, especially combined with warming aromatic herbs like thyme and hyssop.” That interview does not publish her exact syrup recipe or a child’s measured dose, so none is attributed here. Source: Paul Bergner, “Allium sativum: Practitioner interviews,” medherb.com.

Susun Weed’s everyday flu-prevention list simply says: “Eat garlic. (Even powdered, even cooked.) Eat ginger. (Or drink it.)” Source: Susun S. Weed, “Ten Top Tips for Avoiding the Flu,” Wise Woman Herbal Ezine, March 2006, susunweed.com.

Ginger (Zingiber officinale)

Part used: Rhizome.

Energetics: Warming, stimulating, somewhat drying.

Main actions: Traditionally carminative, meaning it helps with gas and digestive discomfort. It has also been studied for nausea, though effects depend on the condition and preparation.

Best forms: Fresh-root decoction, food, or a ginger-containing tea.

Preparation tip: Slice thinly or grate before simmering. Cover the pot to retain aroma.

Conservative food portions: Adults, approximately 1–4 g fresh root; ages 2–6, 0.25–0.5 g in food or a weak tea; ages 6–12, approximately 0.5–1 g in food or weak tea. Pediatric tea use is based on Romm's general guidance, not an established safe daily gram limit.

Safety: [Verified] The AHPA handbook advises professional supervision with active gallstone disease. Ginger may cause heartburn, particularly in larger amounts. A human study found increased antiplatelet activity when ginger was combined with nifedipine. Pregnancy studies of specific ginger preparations exist, but pregnant readers should use condition-specific guidance rather than assuming every ginger extract is equivalent.

Pairs with: Lemon, honey when age-appropriate, mild broths.

Avoid: Concentrated ginger products with anticoagulants or cardiovascular medications without an interaction review; excessive amounts if reflux worsens.

From the tradition Added

Rosalee de la Forêt calls fresh ginger warming and drying, and dried ginger hot and drying. She notes that it is aromatic and dispersing, and that herbalists use it for colds and flu, nausea, inflammatory pain such as arthritis, and menstrual cramps due to stagnation. Her published dosage suggestions are: fresh root 1–15 grams; dried root 3–12 grams; and, citing David Winston and Merrily Kuhn, a fresh tincture at 1:2 in 60% alcohol, 1–2 mL in water three times a day. She cautions that large amounts should not be used during pregnancy, and that people on blood-thinning medication should consult a clinician before taking large amounts regularly. Source: Rosalee de la Forêt, “Ginger Uses and Plant Profile,” LearningHerbs, 14 June 2021, learningherbs.com/blog/ginger-uses, citing Kuhn and Winston, Herbal Therapy & Supplements.

Aviva Romm gives a household ginger tea: steep 1 tablespoon of fresh grated ginger in 1 cup of boiling water for ten minutes, cover while steeping, then add lemon and honey to taste. She says to drink it hot, up to 4 cups daily, and that it is “safe during pregnancy up to 2 cups daily, and while breastfeeding.” She also describes ginger as helpful for aches, pains, digestive symptoms, and congestion that come with fever, colds, and flu. Source: Aviva Romm, “8 Safe and Effective Herbal Antibiotic Alternatives,” avivaromm.com/herbal-antibiotic-alternatives.

The Herbal Academy lists ginger among circulatory stimulants and antispasmodics used in warm compresses and fomentations for muscle discomfort. Source: Angela Justis, “Herbal Compresses and Fomentations,” Herbal Academy, 30 January 2018, theherbalacademy.com, also citing Rosemary Gladstar, Rosemary Gladstar’s Medicinal Herbs: A Beginner’s Guide (2012), on rotating hot and cold compresses as a fomentation.

Turmeric (Curcuma longa)

Part used: Rhizome.

Energetics: Warming and drying in many Western herbal traditions.

Main actions: Traditional digestive and joint-support herb. Curcuminoids have anti-inflammatory activity in laboratory research, but culinary turmeric cannot be assumed equivalent to concentrated clinical extracts.

Best forms: Culinary powder, fresh rhizome, warm milk, soups, and curries.

Preparation tip: Use turmeric with a little dietary fat. Black pepper can increase curcumin exposure, but concentrated piperine supplements are not appropriate to add casually to medicines.

Conservative food portions: Adults, approximately 0.5–1 g powder; ages 2–6, a culinary pinch around 0.1 g; ages 6–12, approximately 0.2–0.3 g in food. No medicinal pediatric daily dose is established here.

Safety: [Verified] The 2013 AHPA handbook classifies the whole herb as relatively low concern in its reviewed uses, but later reports identify rare liver injuries associated with some concentrated turmeric/curcuminoid products. The 2026 HerbalGram review notes that the causal evidence is not conclusive in every case and that normal food use is distinct from supplements.

Avoid medicinal turmeric if you have gallbladder obstruction, biliary disease, or liver disease unless your clinician approves. Review blood thinners and other medications before using concentrated extracts. Do not treat culinary turmeric as equivalent to high-bioavailability curcumin capsules.

Pairs with: Milk or another fat-containing food, ginger in cooking.

Avoid: High-dose curcumin-piperine combinations without professional guidance.

From the tradition Added

Rosalee de la Forêt also profiles turmeric as a pungent kitchen herb in Alchemy of Herbs (2017). The original guide already covers conservative culinary use, the Botanical Safety Handbook, and later turmeric-safety reporting; no additional internal dose is added here.

Ceylon cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum)

Part used: Inner bark.

Energetics: Warming and drying.

Main actions: Aromatic digestive spice and traditional warming circulatory herb. Its use in metabolic health is being researched, but cinnamon is not a substitute for diabetes medication.

Best forms: Lightly powdered spice, cooking, or modest bark infusions.

Preparation tip: Choose products labeled Cinnamomum verum or Cinnamomum zeylanicum.

Conservative food portions: Adults, 0.3–0.5 g daily when used in food; ages 2–6, approximately 0.1 g; ages 6–12, approximately 0.2 g. These are practical culinary portions, not proven therapeutic doses.

Ceylon versus cassia: [Verified] Cassia cinnamon can contain substantially more coumarin, a substance that raises liver safety concerns at sufficiently high repeated exposure. True Ceylon cinnamon is generally the better choice for frequent use. Thin, numerous papery layers in a quill suggest Ceylon; thick single-layer sticks suggest cassia. Powdered cinnamon cannot reliably be identified by appearance alone.

Safety: AHPA lists higher therapeutic amounts of Ceylon cinnamon as inappropriate in pregnancy without practitioner supervision; it distinguishes these from ordinary cooking use. Cinnamon may irritate the mouth or skin. Anyone using blood-glucose-lowering medicines should discuss medicinal cinnamon and monitor glucose.

Pairs with: Oats, apples, ginger, milk.

Avoid: Large daily cassia doses, cinnamon essential oil internally, and concentrated cinnamon therapy alongside diabetes medication without review.

From the tradition Added

Rosalee de la Forêt includes cinnamon among the pungent, warming kitchen spices in Alchemy of Herbs (Hay House, 2017), grouping it with garlic, ginger, rosemary, and thyme. Source: LearningHerbs book page, learningherbs.com/alchemy-of-herbs.

Felter and Lloyd record official aromatic powder made with equal parts Ceylon cinnamon and ginger (with cardamom and nutmeg), traditionally used for flatulent colic, nausea, and diarrhea, at a historical dose of 5 to 20 grains. That is a historical Eclectic dose, not a modern household serving, and is noted here for tradition only. Source: King’s American Dispensatory, henriettes-herb.com/eclectic/kings/cinnamomum_pulv.html.

Rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus)

Part used: Leaves and tender flowering tops.

Energetics: Warming, drying, aromatic.

Main actions: Traditional digestive and circulatory support, culinary antioxidant source, and aromatic ingredient for topical preparations.

Best forms: Cooking, covered infusion, and properly prepared topical compress.

Preparation tip: Crush the leaves lightly before steeping. Use covered infusions to retain aroma.

Conservative food portions: Adults, about 0.25–0.5 g dried leaves; ages 2–6, a small pinch around 0.1 g; ages 6–12, approximately 0.2 g. These refer to food seasoning, not medicinal tea doses.

Safety: [Verified] AHPA distinguishes culinary use from medicinal use and lists rosemary as Safety Class 1 in its handbook, with cautions concerning traditional emmenagogue claims and some theoretical glucose and iron-related effects. Evidence does not establish these theoretical effects as clinically significant at normal food amounts.

Avoid medicinal rosemary during pregnancy or breastfeeding without professional assessment. Test topical preparations on a small area first. Concentrated rosemary essential oil is not interchangeable with the leaf.

Pairs with: Olive oil, garlic, legumes, roasted vegetables.

Avoid: Undiluted essential oil on the skin and ingestion of essential oil.

From the tradition Added

David Hoffmann describes rosemary as “a circulatory and nervine stimulant,” used where psychological tension is present, and says that externally it may be used to ease muscular pain, sciatica, and neuralgia. His infusion directions are: pour a cup of boiling water onto 1–2 teaspoonfuls of the dried herb, cover, infuse 10–15 minutes, and drink three times a day; tincture, 1–2 mL three times a day. Source: David L. Hoffmann, “Rosemary,” healthy.net/2000/12/06/rosemary-3.

Rosalee de la Forêt gives rosemary’s energetics as warming and drying, and its properties as aromatic, carminative, circulatory stimulant, hepatic, antimicrobial, and stimulating. Source: “Rosemary Uses and Plant Profile,” LearningHerbs, learningherbs.com/blog/rosemary-uses.

Susun Weed includes thyme and rosemary among “hot herbal antibacterial teas” in her everyday flu-prevention advice, alongside eating garlic and ginger. Source: “Ten Top Tips for Avoiding the Flu,” March 2006, susunweed.com.

Thyme (Thymus vulgaris)

Part used: Leaves and flowering aerial parts.

Energetics: Warming, drying, aromatic.

Main actions: Traditional respiratory and digestive support. Aromatic constituents such as thymol have laboratory antimicrobial activity, but thyme tea is not a treatment for pneumonia or bacterial throat infection.

Best forms: Covered tea, soup, culinary seasoning.

Preparation tip: Steep covered rather than boiling the leaves vigorously.

Conservative food portions: Adults, approximately 0.25–0.5 g dried herb in cooking; ages 2–6, around 0.1 g; ages 6–12, around 0.2 g. These are culinary amounts only.

Safety: [Verified] The AHPA handbook classifies medicinal thyme use as Safety Class 2b and advises against medicinal use in pregnancy unless supervised. It notes that these cautions do not generally apply to normal food seasoning amounts. Allergic reactions, including sensitivity shared with rosemary or oregano, have been reported. Breastfeeding safety at medicinal doses is insufficiently established.

Pairs with: Honey for an age-appropriate throat drink, garlic, lemon, broth.

Avoid: Thyme essential oil internally and high-strength thyme preparations in pregnancy or young children.

5. Herbal remedies by body system

Every formula below states a single-batch preparation. Unless noted, use clean drinking water, clean utensils, and accurately identified food-grade or herb-grade plant material.

5.1 Digestive system

Focus herb 1: Ginger. [Traditional, with some modern clinical support] Ginger is a useful first-choice kitchen herb for occasional nausea, mild gas, and unsettled digestion. It is easy to prepare at low strength.

Focus herb 2: Fennel. [Traditional] The aromatic fruits, commonly called seeds, have a long history of use after meals for gas and bloating. I have kept medicinal fennel out of the pediatric recipes because of uncertainty about its hormonal activity and long-term safety in young children.

Remedy 1. Ginger settling decoction

Form

Warm decoction.

Ingredients

3 g fresh thinly sliced ginger and 240 mL water.

Preparation

Place ginger and water in a covered saucepan. Bring to a gentle simmer for 10 minutes, remove from heat, rest 3 minutes, then strain.

Use and duration

Adults may drink 60–120 mL slowly once or twice daily for up to 2 days for occasional mild discomfort. Stop when symptoms resolve; if discomfort persists, seek assessment rather than continuing.

Children

Ages 2–6, use a weaker batch made with 1 g fresh ginger in 240 mL water and offer up to 60 mL once daily. Ages 6–12, up to 120 mL of that weaker batch once daily. These are cautious adaptations of Romm's ginger-tea guidance, not validated pediatric doses. Under 2: not recommended without pediatric direction.

Storage

Refrigerate promptly, use within 24 hours.

Safety and pairing

Can pair with plain toast or broth. Avoid if ginger worsens reflux. Seek professional advice with active gallstones, nifedipine, anticoagulants, or bleeding disorders. Possible side effects include heartburn and mouth irritation.
Preparation and adult dose context: Michael Moore, Herbal Materia Medica, ginger decoction. Pediatric context: Aviva Romm, Who's Afraid of Fever?. Safety: Gardner and McGuffin, Botanical Safety Handbook.

Remedy 2. Fennel after-meal infusion

Form

Seed infusion.

Ingredients

1.5 g lightly crushed dried fennel seeds and 240 mL water.

Preparation

Lightly crush the seeds. Cover with freshly boiled water, steep 15 minutes, and strain.

Use and duration

Adults, 60–120 mL after a meal, up to twice daily for 1–2 days. Stop if discomfort worsens or continues.

Children

Ages 2–6 and 6–12: not recommended as a medicinal tea. Under 2: not recommended. Ordinary small seasoning amounts in age-appropriate food are a separate matter.

Storage

Refrigerate, discard after 24 hours.

Safety and pairing

Pairs with light meals. Avoid with known fennel, celery, or related pollen-spice allergies. Use caution with hormonal conditions, pregnancy, breastfeeding, and medicines with important absorption requirements. Fennel may cause allergic symptoms or stomach upset. Its safety profile includes a small case series involving premature breast development in children given large amounts of fennel tea for many months; this does not establish causation for ordinary seasoning.
Dose context: Michael Moore, Herbal Materia Medica, fennel infusion. Safety: Gardner and McGuffin, Botanical Safety Handbook.

5.2 Immune system

Focus herb 1: Garlic. [Traditional] Garlic is a useful culinary herb during ordinary meals, including when appetite is reduced by a mild respiratory illness. Eating garlic has not been proven to cure infections.

Focus herb 2: Elderflower. [Traditional] Elderflowers have a history of use in warm teas during common colds and feverish illnesses. Comfort and hydration are the realistic goals here, not guaranteed immune stimulation.

Remedy 3. Garlic-lemon comfort broth

Form

Culinary broth.

Ingredients

1 fresh garlic clove (approximately 3 g), 500 mL low-sodium vegetable broth, and 10 mL fresh lemon juice.

Preparation

Suggested extra focus herb: Thyme herbal steam Added

This is a separate respiratory preparation from Remedy 5 (thyme-honey throat tea) and Remedy 6 (marshmallow cold infusion). Thyme is a traditional expectorant and cough herb in Hoffmann and in King’s American Dispensatory. The method below follows the Herbal Academy’s decongesting herbal steam, simplified to pantry thyme, which the Academy says you may do with herbs you have on hand. Essential oils are omitted in this version.

Form

Inhaled herbal steam, not swallowed.

Ingredients

1–3 tablespoons dried thyme (the Academy’s blended recipe uses 3 tablespoons thyme among other herbs; a simple thyme-only bowl uses the same water volume). 4–6 cups water, brought to a boil. A large heat-safe bowl and a towel.

Preparation

Place the thyme in a large bowl. Pour 4–6 cups of boiling water over the herb and cover the bowl briefly. Sit with a towel over your head to hold the steam. A short steam during ordinary congestion, as described by the Herbal Academy. This loosens mucus and moistens the airways; it is not a treatment for pneumonia or severe breathing difficulty, which need medical care (see the red-flag section).

Storage

Prepare at the time of use and discard the water after it cools.

Safety and pairing

Burn risk from steam and hot water is the main household hazard. Stop if you feel lightheaded, overheated, or irritated. People with asthma or very reactive airways should be cautious with concentrated aromatic steams and stop if breathing worsens. Seek urgent care for serious breathing trouble rather than continuing a steam.

Suggested extra focus herb: Warm ginger compress Added

This is a separate musculoskeletal preparation from Remedy 11 (turmeric golden milk) and Remedy 12 (rosemary warm joint compress). It gives ginger a topical role alongside its digestive tea in Remedy 1. The method follows the Herbal Academy’s general compress instructions; the Academy specifically lists ginger as a circulatory stimulant and antispasmodic used to help with muscle discomfort, and describes a family example that used powdered ginger in a strong infusion applied as a hot fomentation.

Form

Warm topical compress (a cloth soaked in a strong ginger tea).

Ingredients

3 to 4 tablespoons freshly grated ginger, or powdered ginger, per 1 cup (240 mL) water, which is the Academy’s general strength for compress teas. A clean cotton cloth. Optional: a towel and a comfortably warm water bottle if you are making a fomentation (a compress kept warm).

5.9 Integumentary system: skin

Focus herb 1: Calendula. [Traditional] Calendula has a long history in external preparations intended to comfort dry or mildly irritated skin. It is well suited to a beginner salve project.

Focus herb 2: Oats. [Traditional and modern dermatologic use] Finely dispersed oats can provide a soothing bath or soak for intact, itchy, dry skin.

Remedy 17. Calendula skin salve

Form

Infused oil and salve.

Ingredients

10 g fully dried calendula petals or flowers, 100 mL olive oil, and 10 g beeswax.

Preparation

1. Combine dried calendula and olive oil in a heat-safe vessel set over gently heated water.
2. Warm indirectly over low heat for 1–2 hours, avoiding boiling oil or introducing water.
3. Strain thoroughly through clean, dry cloth.
4. Place the infused oil back over gentle indirect heat and melt in beeswax.
5. Pour into clean, dry tins and allow to cool.

Use and duration

Adults, a thin layer on a small area of dry, intact skin once or twice daily for up to 5 days. Stop if irritation develops.

Children

Ages 2–6, a very small smear, approximately rice-grain to pea size depending on the area. Ages 6–12, a pea-sized thin application. Topical amounts are practical application guidance, not clinically validated pediatric herbal doses. Patch-test first. For under 2, plain fragrance-free moisturizer is preferable without pediatric direction.

Storage

In a clean, closed container away from heat and light, a conservative 2–3 months if made without introducing water. Discard sooner if smell, appearance, or texture changes.

Safety and pairing

Pairs with gentle cleansing and fragrance-free skincare. Avoid calendula/Asteraceae allergy, infected skin, deep wounds, and severe burns. Possible contact dermatitis. Discuss use on damaged skin, during pregnancy, or on infants with a healthcare professional.
Oil and salve technique: Jim McDonald, Herbal Preparations. Safety: Gardner and McGuffin, Botanical Safety Handbook, calendula monograph.

Remedy 18. Soothing oat bath

Form

Localized bath or soak.

Ingredients

30 g finely ground plain oats in a clean cloth pouch, and approximately 10 L lukewarm water in a basin.

Preparation

Grind oats finely, preferably using a clean grinder. Place in a cloth pouch, suspend in lukewarm water, and squeeze gently for 2 minutes until the water looks milky. Soak the affected hands, feet, or other suitable area for 10 minutes. Pat dry and apply a bland moisturizer.

Use and duration

Adults, once daily for up to 3 days when needed.

Children

Ages 2–6, use 15 g oats in 10 L water and soak for 5–10 minutes with supervision. Ages 6–12, 25 g oats in 10 L and soak 5–10 minutes. These are practical bath concentrations, not measured medicinal doses. Under 2: seek pediatric advice for persistent skin conditions.

Storage

Single use only. Discard bath water and oats immediately.

Safety and pairing

Pairs with fragrance-free moisturizers. Avoid with known oat allergy or irritated skin that reacts to the preparation. Choose certified gluten-free oats if cross-contact with gluten is a relevant concern. Be careful because oats can make the basin slippery. Stop for hives, increased redness, pain, or worsening skin symptoms.
Botanical safety: Gardner and McGuffin, Botanical Safety Handbook, Avena sativa monograph. The bath concentrations are conservative original home formulations.

5.10 Endocrine and metabolic system

This section focuses on food patterns that are compatible with everyday metabolic health, not treatment of thyroid disease, diabetes, adrenal disease, or hormone disorders.

Focus herb 1: Ceylon cinnamon. [Traditional and modern study] Cinnamon is being studied for metabolic effects, but evidence does not justify replacing glucose-lowering medicine with cinnamon, particularly at normal food doses.

Focus herb 2: Flaxseed. [Food and modern nutritional research] Ground flaxseed contributes fiber and plant lignans. Its practical value in this guide is as a food ingredient, not a proven hormone-balancing medication.

Remedy 19. Ceylon cinnamon oat bowl

Form

Culinary preparation.

Ingredients

40 g rolled oats, 240 mL water, and 0.3 g ground Ceylon cinnamon.

Preparation

Place ingredients in a small saucepan. Simmer for 5–7 minutes, stirring until the oats soften. Allow to cool.

Use and duration

Adults, one bowl at breakfast, as desired. No scheduled herbal break is needed when used as ordinary food.

Children

Ages 2–6, prepare with 20 g oats, 120 mL water, and 0.1 g Ceylon cinnamon. Ages 6–12, 30 g oats, 180 mL water, and 0.2 g cinnamon. These are culinary portions rather than pediatric medicinal doses. Under 2, use age-appropriate plain oats, with any spice introduced as normal food.

Storage

Eat fresh or refrigerate up to 24 hours and reheat thoroughly.

Safety and pairing

Pairs with nuts or fruit where age-appropriate and allergy-safe. Avoid cinnamon allergy. If taking diabetes medicines, do not rely on cinnamon to adjust glucose medication. Choose Ceylon rather than high-coumarin cassia for frequent use. Possible mouth irritation or allergy.
Botanical cautions: Gardner and McGuffin, Botanical Safety Handbook, Ceylon cinnamon monograph. Food amounts and recipe are original culinary guidance.

Remedy 20. Flaxseed yogurt bowl

Form

Culinary preparation.

Ingredients

5 g freshly ground flaxseed, 150 g plain yogurt, and 50 mL water.

Preparation

Stir the ground flaxseed into yogurt and water. Rest 10 minutes to allow it to hydrate, then eat.

Use and duration

Adults, once daily for 3–5 days while assessing digestive tolerance. It may remain a normal food thereafter if well tolerated.

Children

Ages 2–6, 1 g ground flaxseed mixed into 75 g yogurt and 20 mL water. Ages 6–12, 2 g ground flaxseed mixed into 100 g yogurt and 30 mL water. Food portions only. Under 2, discuss suitable textures and ingredients with a pediatric clinician if needed.

5.11 Reproductive system: female and male considerations

Focus herb 1: Raspberry leaf, female considerations. [Traditional] Raspberry leaf is commonly used in Western herbal practice around menstruation and women's reproductive health. Evidence for relieving menstrual discomfort is limited, and pregnancy use needs individualized review.

Focus herb 2: Pumpkin seed, male considerations. [Food and modern research interest] Pumpkin seed is a nutrient-dense food. Some studies have evaluated pumpkin seed extracts or oils for urinary symptoms associated with benign prostatic enlargement, but those findings cannot be directly transferred to ordinary roasted seeds.

Remedy 21. Raspberry leaf cycle tea

Form

Leaf infusion.

Ingredients

2 g dried red raspberry leaf and 240 mL water.

Preparation

Pour hot water over the leaves, cover for 20 minutes, and strain.

Use and duration

Nonpregnant adults, 60–120 mL once daily for up to 3 days during the menstrual period, then stop. This is a traditional use, not a clinically proven treatment for painful periods.

Children

Ages 2–6 and 6–12: not recommended. For menstruating adolescents, seek age-specific guidance rather than adopting the adult formula. Under 2: not recommended.

Storage

Refrigerate up to 24 hours.

Pairs with adequate hydration and appropriate non-drug comfort measures. Avoid self-directed medicinal use if pregnant, trying to induce labor, or breastfeeding without clinical guidance. Consult for heavy bleeding, severe period pain, endometriosis concerns, or medication interactions. Possible digestive upset and allergy.

Remedy 22. Pumpkin seed nourishment topping

Form

Culinary preparation.

Ingredients

15 g shelled pumpkin seeds, 150 g cooked vegetables or salad, and 5 mL olive oil.

Preparation

Warm pumpkin seeds in a dry skillet over low heat for 2–3 minutes, stirring and avoiding scorching. Allow to cool and scatter over vegetables with olive oil.

Use and duration

Adults, one serving daily as food for 3–5 days, or regularly as part of a varied diet. No medicinal cycle is required.

Children

Choosing good-quality herbs

Organic: Organic sourcing can reduce certain pesticide concerns, but organic status alone does not verify botanical identity, microbial cleanliness, or heavy-metal safety.

Fresh versus dried: Fresh ginger and garlic should feel firm, without mold or sliminess. Dried leaves and flowers should retain recognizable color and aroma, and should not smell musty. Discard herbs that are damp, moldy, or contaminated.

Turmeric: Look for a clearly identified Curcuma longa product, preferably from a supplier that can document identity and contaminant testing. Color alone cannot confirm quality. [Verified] The American Botanical Council's adulteration reporting describes contamination and adulteration concerns involving turmeric powder, including prohibited dyes and lead chromate.

Cinnamon: Choose labeling that explicitly identifies Cinnamomum verum or C. zeylanicum. “Cinnamon” alone often does not identify the species.

Buying online: Prefer sellers that give the Latin botanical name, plant part, harvest or batch identification, and testing information. Buy quantities you can use while aromatic quality remains good, rather than stocking a multi-year supply.

When home remedies are not enough

Get emergency help for: Chest pain, severe trouble breathing, stroke symptoms, fainting with concerning symptoms, severe allergic reactions, uncontrolled bleeding, or severe sudden abdominal pain. Call 911 in the United States.

Seek prompt medical evaluation for: Fever with severe headache, stiff neck, dehydration, persistent vomiting, rapidly worsening illness, painful swollen joints with fever, urinary burning with fever or back pain, blood in urine or stool, or severe menstrual or pelvic pain.

Special considerations for children: Fever in a baby under 3 months, breathing difficulty, reduced responsiveness, or significant dehydration requires urgent medical evaluation. Do not delay care to trial herbal teas.

Seek individualized advice before medicinal herbs if you: Are pregnant or breastfeeding; take anticoagulants, antiplatelet medicines, diabetes or blood-pressure medication; have liver, kidney, or gallbladder disease; or have an upcoming surgery. Normal food seasoning does not necessarily have the same risks as concentrated extracts.

Stop a new herb if you develop a rash, wheezing, dizziness, persistent nausea, unusual bruising, or another unexpected symptom. Seek medical attention as appropriate.

Final recommendation

Use the guide as a small-batch, one-remedy-at-a-time learning reference. Its strongest beginner material is the gentle food-based preparations, selected mild teas, and intact-skin topical recipes. The lymphatic, urinary, endocrine, and reproductive sections require the most caution about overinterpreting traditional claims.

If you follow only three principles, make them these: identify every plant correctly, keep medicinal preparations separate from ordinary culinary use when assessing risk, and never calculate a child's herbal dose from an adult dose unless the plant and preparation are independently established as appropriate for pediatric use.
